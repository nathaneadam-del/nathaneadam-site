Tonight with Deborah Norville

Producing and editing for MSNBC’s nightly show, then sitting on the other side of the camera when the network wanted pitch correction explained to a national audience.

Singing or Synching?

By 2004 the general public had worked out that the singing on their favourite records was being fixed in software, and reacted roughly the way you would expect. Was it cheating? Could anyone actually sing any more? MSNBC ran the question as a segment, and needed somebody who did the work to explain it on camera.

That was two years before the book, which is the right order for it to have happened in. The argument arrived first and in public. The book is the long answer, written for the people doing the work rather than the people watching it on television.

That was me, in a studio, in front of a console. The answer I gave then is still the one I give: the tool is not the argument. Pitch correction had been in Nashville sessions for years by that point, doing quietly and quickly what tape splicing and punch-ins had been doing slowly and expensively for decades. What changed was the speed, not the ethics.

A YouTube upload of the segments passed 820,000 views before it came down. The broadcast itself is more than twenty years old now and isn't online anywhere I can find, so that figure is the last count I have and there's nothing left to point you at. I'd rather say so plainly than leave the number sitting there looking sourced.

Why it keeps happening

I have now had a version of this conversation on television three times across twenty years, and only the technology in the question changes. In 2023 NewsChannel 5 and WPLN asked the same thing about generative AI in music: is this cheating, and is it the end of the job. It is the same segment. Being the person who had already answered it once about pitch correction is most of why they call.
