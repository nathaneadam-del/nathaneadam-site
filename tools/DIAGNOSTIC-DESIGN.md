# The career pivot diagnostic — content design

Draft 1, 1 September 2026. **BUILT AND SHIPPED the same day.** Nathan reviewed
this draft, said "I like it for now," and it went live as written.

**This file is the reasoning, not the copy.** The words that actually render are
in `content.js`, and that file is the one to edit. If somebody changes a read
there and not here, `content.js` wins and this doc is just stale history. Worth
keeping the two roughly in step, but don't treat this as the source.

The open questions at the bottom were deliberately left open rather than settled,
because they're the kind of thing that only gets properly answered once real
people have gone through the tool.

Read `CLAUDE.md` first for the editorial rules. They apply to every word below,
and one of them is doing a lot of work here: **no invented numbers.** A tool that
tells somebody "your role is 68% exposed" has made that up. There is no study
behind it and there never was. So this thing does not score anyone, does not
produce a percentage, and does not hand out a personality type.

What it does instead is name what is exposed in their work, name what is not,
say why, and frame the whole thing as Nathan's read rather than a finding. That
is defensible. A number is not.

---

## The framework

This is not borrowed from anywhere. It is Nathan's own career, generalised, and
it is the honest answer to the homepage headline.

Work gets **exposed** when it is:

- executing somebody else's spec
- judged by technical criteria that can be checked
- sourced through a platform or a marketplace
- priced per deliverable
- describable in words well enough for a stranger to attempt it

Work stays **durable** when it involves:

- deciding what "good" means, rather than hitting a target somebody else set
- being trusted by a specific person who came looking for you
- being in the room, or being the one who is accountable when it goes wrong
- being able to teach it to somebody else

The reason Nathan can say this without a citation is that he watched it happen
once already. The million-dollar console did not lose to a better console. It
lost sessions to a $2,500 computer running Pro Tools LE. The engineers who came
through that fine were not the ones with the fastest hands.

## Structure

Ten screens on a phone, one question per screen, big tap targets, a progress
line. First question branches. Answers go to `localStorage`. Partial result is
free. Full result is behind the beehiiv subscribe.

**Question 1 (the branch)**

> Where are you right now?
>
> - I've been working in my field for a while → **Track A**
> - I'm early on. Studying, or a few years in → **Track B**

## Track A — mid-career

Nine questions. A2 through A6 and A9 produce the read. A7 and A8 do not change
the diagnosis, they change the recommendation.

**A1. What's the core of your work?**
Audio and music · Video and film · Photography · Design and visual · Writing ·
Teaching and training · Something else

*(Flavours the language in the result. Does not score.)*

**A2. On a typical paid job, who decides what "good" means?**
- The client, and they're specific about it → exposed
- We work it out together → middle
- Me. That's what they're paying for → durable

**A3. Think about your last ten jobs. Where did they come from?**
- A platform, a marketplace, or a job board → exposed
- A mix of both
- People who already knew me, or who were sent by people who knew me → durable

**A4. Could a smart client describe what they want, in words, well enough that a
stranger could have a decent go at it?**
- Yes, pretty much → exposed
- Roughly, but they'd get it wrong in ways that matter
- No. Half my job is working out what they actually want → durable

**A5. What are you actually paid for?**
- The deliverable. So many files, so many minutes, so many pages → exposed
- My time
- The outcome, or for being the one responsible when it goes wrong → durable

**A6. If you could produce three times as much work in a week, what happens to
what you earn?**
- It'd roughly triple → exposed
- It'd go up somewhat
- Not much. My rate was never about volume → durable

**A7. Have you used AI tools on work you actually got paid for?**
- No, not yet
- Tried it. Didn't stick
- Yes. It's part of how I work now

**A8. Does anybody learn your craft from you? Students, juniors, a channel, a
client you've trained up?**
- No
- Informally, now and then
- Yes, regularly

**A9. How much of a job is you being in the room, or being personally on the
hook?**
- Almost none. I could do all of it from anywhere, and I usually do → exposed
- Some of it
- A lot of it → durable

## The three reads (Track A)

Majority of exposed answers → Read 1. Majority durable → Read 3. Anything else,
which will be most people → Read 2.

**Nobody gets told they're fine.** Read 3 is not a pat on the head, it is a
different risk. That is the part that makes this worth doing.

### Read 1 — "You're being paid to execute"

*(Free portion, shown to everyone)*

> Right now you're being paid for the doing, not the deciding.
>
> Your jobs come in with the answer already attached. Somebody else has decided
> what the thing should be and you're the person who makes it exist, on time, to
> spec, without mistakes. You are probably very good at this. That is not the
> problem.

*(Gated portion)*

> The problem is that "on time, to spec, without mistakes" is the exact
> description of what machines got good at this decade. Not the taste. The
> execution.
>
> Here's what I'd want you to notice though, because the way this normally gets
> written up is basically a doom letter and that isn't the honest version. You
> already have the thing that survives this. You know what good is. You can
> listen to two versions and tell me which one is right, and probably tell me
> why. That took years and you can't download it. It's just that nobody is
> currently paying you for it, and you have never asked them to.
>
> The gap between what you know and what you're paid for is the whole of your
> risk, and the whole of your opportunity. Same gap.

### Read 2 — "Your work is a bundle, and half of it just got cheaper"

*(Free portion)*

> You're in the middle, and the middle is where the ground is moving.
>
> Most people land here, so don't read that as a soft landing. It's just where a
> working craft career actually sits right now.

*(Gated portion)*

> You sell one price for one project. Inside that price there are two different
> jobs. There's deciding what the thing should be, what stays, what goes, what's
> actually wrong with it. And there's sitting there and doing it.
>
> For a long time those were welded together, and for a good reason. The only
> way to get the judgment was to put in the hours doing the execution. The only
> person who could execute at a decent level was somebody who had already earned
> the judgment. So we priced them as one thing, because they arrived as one
> thing.
>
> That weld is coming apart. And here's the part that gets people, because it
> doesn't announce itself. Nobody sends you an email saying the execution half of
> your bundle is worth less now. What happens is your rate quietly stops moving,
> a job you'd have got two years ago goes somewhere else, and a client asks a
> question about your quote that they'd never have asked before. It reads like a
> slow patch. It isn't.
>
> If you price the bundle as one thing, you'll get benchmarked on the half that
> got cheap.

### Read 3 — "You're already selling judgment, and that's not the same as being safe"

*(Free portion)*

> You're mostly being paid to decide, not to do. Clients come to you by name,
> they can't fully specify what they want, and you're the one on the hook when
> it goes sideways.
>
> That's the durable end of this. It is not the safe end, and those are different
> things.

*(Gated portion)*

> I'll be straight about the temptation here. Everything you just told me points
> the good way, and the easy thing for me to do is tell you you're fine and let
> you get on with your day. You're not exactly fine, you're just facing a
> different problem than the one everybody's writing about.
>
> Your risk isn't that a machine does your job. Your risk is somebody who sells
> the same judgment you do, with faster and cheaper execution running underneath
> it, and who therefore gets to say yes to three times as much work as you do.
> Same taste. Better throughput. They'll take the jobs you were going to take,
> not because they're better than you but because they answered first and quoted
> lower and it still cleared their bar.
>
> Judgment is the moat. Nobody said the moat came with speed attached.

## Track B — early career

Different questions, because they have no client history to reason from, and a
different message. The honest one, which is not the one they usually get.

**B1.** What are you training in or aiming at? *(same list as A1)*

**B2.** What pulled you into it?
- I'm good at the technical side of it
- I like deciding what's good and what isn't
- I like working with people on their stuff

**B3.** When you finish something, how do you know if it's any good?
- It meets the brief and it's technically clean
- I hold it up against work I admire
- Somebody I trust tells me

**B4.** Right now, is most of your learning about the tools, or about the
judgment?
- Mostly learning the software
- A mix
- Mostly learning to hear or see the difference

**B5.** Have you made anything for a real person yet? Paid or not.
- Not yet
- One or two things
- Regularly

**B6.** Is there anyone who'd vouch for you by name?
- Not really
- One or two people
- Several

**B7.** AI tools?
- Steering clear
- Messing about with them
- Already using them in my work

**B8.** Two years from now, which would bother you more?
- Being slower than everyone else
- Not knowing whether my work is any good
- Nobody knowing who I am

### The Track B read

One read with three endings, because the diagnosis is the same for all of them
and only the fix changes.

*(Free portion)*

> I'm going to tell you the thing people your age keep getting told sideways.
>
> The bottom rung is being sawn off. The job that used to exist so somebody could
> learn on it, the assisting, the log-and-transfer, the first pass that a senior
> person then fixed, that job is going away, and it is going away fastest in
> exactly the fields people go to school for.

*(Gated portion)*

> Everyone hears that as "you're doomed." It isn't what it means. Read it again.
> The rung that's disappearing is the *learning* rung. Not the career. The
> apprenticeship.
>
> That matters because of how the old deal worked. You did four years of
> unglamorous execution and the judgment arrived as a byproduct. Nobody handed it
> to you, you absorbed it, mostly by watching somebody senior reject things and
> occasionally by being the one who got it wrong. It was slow, it was badly paid,
> and it worked.
>
> Now the unglamorous execution is cheap, so nobody's paying you to sit through
> it. Which means the judgment does not arrive by itself any more. You have to go
> and get it on purpose.
>
> That's the whole thing. That's the actual difference between your generation
> and mine, and almost nobody says it plainly.
>
> The good news, and it is genuinely good, is that the reps got cheaper for you
> too. I had to book a room to hear what a bad decision sounded like. You don't.
> You can make forty versions of something this week and develop an opinion about
> which one is right, which is the thing that used to take four years of somebody
> else's payroll.
>
> Nobody will make you do it. That's the catch. It is the first generation where
> taste has to be deliberate.

## The 90-day move

Driven by A7 / B7, the AI adoption answer, not by the exposure read. One move.
Not five. A list of five is a list nobody does.

**Not using it yet →**

> Pick the one task you'd never show a client. The renaming, the logging, the
> first pass, the transcript, the thing you resent. Do that with AI for thirty
> days and nothing else.
>
> Not the part you care about. I'm being deliberate about that. Almost everyone
> tries this on the work they're proud of, hates the result, decides the tools
> are rubbish, and stops. Which is a shame, because they were right about the
> result and wrong about the conclusion.

**Tried it, didn't stick →**

> It didn't stick because you tried it on the part you care about. That's not a
> character flaw, it's the obvious place to start, it's just the wrong one.
>
> Move it to the part you don't care about and leave it there for a month.

**Already using it →**

> Then your next move isn't a tool, it's your invoice.
>
> Your hours just got shorter. If you're still billing by the hour or by the
> deliverable, you're about to hand the entire gain to your clients and then
> wonder why a good year felt like a flat one. Price one job on the outcome this
> quarter. One. See what happens.
>
> I know that's the least fun advice on this page. :)

## What I wouldn't bother with

Shown to everybody, same copy. This is the section that makes the tool worth
sending to somebody else, because everybody hands out advice and nobody tells
you what to skip.

> - **Learning "prompt engineering" as a skill.** It's a phrasebook for a
>   language that's being redesigned every few months. Learn the craft judgment
>   instead. That transfers, and it has for thirty years.
> - **Rebranding yourself as an AI anything.** The people doing this in 2026 are
>   going to look how "social media guru" looks now.
> - **Trying every new tool.** There's a new one every week. You will spend the
>   whole year evaluating and none of it making.
> - **Waiting for it to settle.** It isn't going to, and that's fine. It didn't
>   settle after Pro Tools either. Everybody who waited for that just missed the
>   part where the work was easy to get.

## The caveat

Sits at the bottom of every result. Non-negotiable, and it's the thing that
keeps this honest.

> One thing before you take any of that too seriously.
>
> This is my read. It isn't a study, there's no data set behind it, and I didn't
> run a survey. It's what twenty-five years of teaching this and a front-row seat
> at the last technology change makes me think, which is worth something and
> isn't worth everything.
>
> I've been wrong about this before, by the way. I was quietly pleased to be
> moving on from shipping a DVD with a book, and I put the material on a
> companion website instead. The website is long gone. The DVD still works in
> every copy that ever sold.
>
> So take the read, argue with it, keep the bit that fits.

## Open questions — raised 1 Sep, deliberately NOT settled

Nathan saw these and shipped anyway, which is the right call for a first
version. They are the things to look at once real people have taken it, not
things that were forgotten.

1. **Read 3's ending.** "Same taste, better throughput" is the sharpest claim on
   the page and the one most likely to annoy an experienced reader. Keep it?
2. **Track B, "the bottom rung is being sawn off."** True as far as I can tell,
   and it's a strong claim on a site with an editorial rule about strong claims.
   Is this your view, said plainly, or does it need softening to "looks like it
   is"?
3. **The Pro Tools LE comparison** runs underneath the whole thing. Do you want
   it stated outright in the intro copy, so people know why you get to have an
   opinion, or left implicit?
4. **A1/B1 craft list.** Right categories? "Something else" needs a sensible
   fallback result and currently doesn't have one.
5. Anything in the reads that doesn't sound like you.
